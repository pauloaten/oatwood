There's a moment — maybe ten minutes into planing a wide oak board — when the shavings start coming off translucent and the surface under your hands feels like cold glass. No belt sander gives you that. No router. Nothing with a plug.

I'm not a Luddite. The bandsaw gets used daily. The thickness planer saves hours. But for final dimensioning and finishing, I reach for planes nine times out of ten.

## Why planes still win for furniture

The main reason is grain reading. A hand plane forces you to pay attention to the direction the fibres are running. Go against the grain and you get tearout. Work with it, and the surface essentially planes itself — the iron slices cleanly under each fibre rather than lifting them.

A random orbital sander doesn't care about grain. It abrades everything equally, which means it also leaves scratches across the grain that are invisible until you apply a finish. Under oil, they glow.

> The best finish is one that starts with the sharpest possible edge. Everything else is just catching up.

## The planes I actually use

- **No. 4 bench plane** — the workhorse. Fits most surfaces, light enough to use one-handed on narrow stock.
- **No. 7 jointer** — for flattening wide boards and shooting long edges true. Heavy, but the mass helps.
- **Shoulder plane** — every tenon shoulder. Non-negotiable.
- **Router plane** — cutting dadoes and housing joints to a consistent depth. Slower than a powered router, but you can hear and feel exactly what's happening.

## Getting started

If you've never tried hand planes, start with a secondhand Stanley No. 4. Flatten the sole, sharpen the iron properly (this is the critical part — a blunt plane is useless), and spend twenty minutes on a piece of pine. You'll understand immediately.

Sharpening is a whole topic in itself, but the short version: work up through grits on waterstones to at least 8000 grit, then strop on leather. The edge should shave arm hair without dragging.

Once you've got the feel for it, you won't want to go back.
